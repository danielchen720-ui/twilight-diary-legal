# Journal with lock — which kind of lock actually keeps it yours

A journal with lock and key used to mean a small brass clasp and a key you hid in a sock drawer. Everyone knew the clasp could be forced. It still worked, because it said one clear thing: this book is mine.

On a phone, "lock" can mean three very different things. If you're looking for a journal with a lock, it helps to know which one you're getting.

## Lock 1: your phone's own lock

Every journal app on your phone sits behind the phone's passcode, Face ID or Touch ID. That keeps out a stranger who finds your phone on a bus.

It doesn't keep out anyone who knows your passcode — and for most people, that list is longer than it sounds. Partners, kids, parents, the friend who borrowed your phone to call a ride. Once the phone is unlocked, every app on it is open, including your journal.

## Lock 2: hiding or locking the app with the phone

Newer versions of iOS let you lock an app or hide it behind Face ID. It's a real improvement: someone scrolling through your home screen won't just tap into your journal.

But the fallback for that lock is your phone's passcode. Anyone who knows the passcode can open the app anyway. It's the same key as Lock 1, on a second door.

## Lock 3: a lock that belongs to the journal

The third kind is a journal with a lock of its own — a code that has nothing to do with your phone.

That's how Diary BFF works. It opens with its own PIN, separate from your phone's passcode. Your phone can be unlocked and in someone else's hand, and the journal still asks for its own code. There's no Face ID or Touch ID shortcut, by design: a face unlock opens for the phone, and a journal needs a lock that opens for the writer.

There's also no sign-up, so there's nothing to recover by logging in somewhere else. The PIN is the lock, and it's the only key.

## What happens if you lose the key

With a brass clasp, losing the key meant a pair of scissors. With Diary BFF, it works like this: you can start a reset with your phone's own device authentication, and the new PIN only unlocks after a 10-day wait. That wait is the point — somebody who knows your phone passcode can't use it to get into your journal today. Your entries stay intact.

## A lock is more than the front door

A journal with a lock should keep your words out of other places, too:

- **Notifications never show what you wrote.** They can appear on a lock screen, so they don't carry your words.
- **Your entries are not used to train AI.**
- **Exports can be locked.** You can export your journal whenever you like, and the backup file can have its own lock.
- **Recently Deleted holds deleted entries for 30 days,** so you can restore them, and then they're gone.

## What's behind this lock

Diary BFF is a night journal with someone in it. You write what's going round in your head, and when you ask, your diary BFF reads it and writes back: a few sentences, at most one question, no lists, no advice unless you ask for it. It remembers what you wrote on earlier nights and tells you which night it's talking about. On Sundays, it writes you a letter about your week.

It doesn't jump in while you write. The writing comes first; the reply only comes when you want one.

## How to choose a journal with a lock

Ask three questions of any app you're considering:

1. **Does it open with my phone's passcode?** If yes, it's Lock 1 or Lock 2.
2. **Is there a separate code that only I know?** That's the lock that holds when the phone is unlocked.
3. **Do notifications show what I wrote?** A locked journal that previews your entry on the lock screen isn't really locked.

Diary BFF answers those as: no, yes, and no.
